Why a chance and not a date

Why tasks take very different amounts of time

I checked 880 software tasks with the same kind of work, the same estimate and the same process. Half were done within three days. One in twenty took longer than four weeks.

It's public data, so you can check it. There was no better estimate to find. Your own history has the chance for every date.

Where does a number like 34% come from? Take the example at the top: 67 items, promised for March 15. I don't ask the team for a better estimate. I take what they finished in the last twelve weeks, fast weeks and slow ones, and play the 67 items forward 10,000 times. 3,400 of those runs are done by March 15. That's the 34%. The realistic date is the one 8,500 runs made.

What happens in the Delivery Audit, from first call to the second measurement

The Delivery Audit costs €4,500 excluding VAT, at a fixed price. It is a written report, but the report is the last step. This is everything before it.

The 14 days

  1. The first call, free, 30 minutes. You tell me about your team, the dates you promised and to whom. We agree which deadlines go in, up to five.

  2. You connect your tracker. It is the same read-only connection as the Scan, or a file export you look through before you send it.

  3. A working session, 45 to 60 minutes. With you, and a team lead if useful. How work gets from idea to shipped, what your statuses really mean and what gets built first.

  4. I go through your numbers. I model how work moves through your team and check how it actually moved before I run anything. Then I work out the chance and the realistic date for every deadline.

  5. Day 14: the walkthrough. We go through the audit together for an hour, and you get it as a PDF right after.

  6. Four weeks later I measure the same things again and write down what moved and what didn't.

What I look at in your tracker

When work moved between statuses, blocker flags, how items group into epics, created and resolved dates, when items were added, and sprint boundaries. I don't use descriptions, comments, attachments or code.

I am looking for three things: where work waits, what was added after the date was promised, and what was started too early. The numbers can tell you a date is at risk. They can't tell you why, so I look.

What I did in two teams, step by step

The results are at the end of each story. What I did comes first, because that is what you would be paying for.

Real estate marketplace for CIB Vlaanderen · first product hire

Spotto: the promise I wouldn't make

Two months after launch the team was me and one developer. I was asked to commit to the quarter's plan: 14 items by 30 June, with at most 10% left undone. I said I couldn't promise it. I had no number, only a feeling.

  1. I found where work stalled. Every time the developers handed work over for review or testing, it came back 6 times in 10.

  2. I gave them one list in one place for questions. The developers were emailing the business side and waiting days. I joined their Slack and became the first stop for every question and bug report.

  3. I defined "done". I wrote the acceptance tests down: scenarios for each type of user, with a worked example of useful feedback. I tested releases myself, and held one back the evening before it was due.

  4. I planned one quarter at a time. A short list, checked every week or two. When an urgent feature request came in, we gave a date six weeks out and named what would be dropped to make room.

  5. I measured three periods in the tracking history: before launch, around it and the months after.

Result. Work that came back after review or testing fell from 61% to 24% to 21%. 13 of the 14 items were accepted by 22 June, eight days early. Three years later I ran a forecast on that team's own record, as it stood on the day I was asked. The plan had an 86% chance of holding.

Factry: how an implementation went from 18 months to 6

Factry builds software that runs on factory floors. Their previous client implementation had taken 18 months and nearly broke the team, and a new client was coming. The technology wasn't the problem. How decisions were made and how work moved was.

  1. I sat with the machine operators who would use the system, on the factory floor.

  2. I built rough prototypes and walked them back through those same operators before a line of production code was written. That removed weeks of rework.

  3. I looked at the daily meeting. It had become an hour-long conversation between one developer and the founder while seven people waited.

  4. We took the founder out of it. Five one-hour meetings a week became fifteen minutes each.

Result. The next comparable client implementation took 6 months instead of 18. Not because of a new framework or tool. Decisions happened faster, the right people were involved at the right time, and the team stopped waiting.

Do you need our estimates or story points?

No. The forecast uses what your team finished and when, not what anyone estimated.

How is this different from the dashboard we already have?

How do I know the number is right?

It isn't an opinion. It's your team's own last twelve weeks, played forward. The method and the data behind it are public, and running it again next month shows whether the date is getting closer.

If you can still move the date or cut scope, yes. You get the chance, the realistic date and how much of each closes the gap, and it's free to run again next month.
